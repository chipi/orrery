The question is often phrased: why Mars? Why send people across one hundred and forty million kilometers of empty sunshine, to a planet that has no breathable air, no liquid water, no magnetic field to shield them from cosmic rays, no fast way home? Mars is not where we are going next. Mars is what we are going next for . Look at the screens in this orrery. Every rover, every orbiter, every lander placed on the Moon since 2007 — Chang'e, Chandrayaan, SLIM, Artemis flight planning — is, in some sense, a rehearsal. Every module of the International Space Station, every Tiangong segment, every long-duration crew that has lived continuously in microgravity for the past quarter-century — is a rehearsal. Every reusable booster lifting payload to orbit at a tenth of the cost it took twenty years ago — is a rehearsal. Mars is the destination that organizes the others. A Moon base teaches us to live off-world without going so far that a week's resupply is impossible. A continuous space station teaches us what a year of microgravity does to muscle, bone, vision, and mind. A rover landed by a robotic teleoperator teaches us autonomy at the distance where conversation cannot. The argument for Mars is not the argument that it is easy. It is not. The argument is that the questions Mars asks have no other answer. Is life common in the universe? Was Mars once Earth-like, and what did it lose, and how? Can a multi-celled biosphere migrate? Can a civilization? Look at this map. Look at the rovers already on it — silent, persistent, slowly tracing across the planet on errands set by teams two hundred million kilometers away. Every one of them is a postcard sent ahead. They are waiting for whoever comes to pick up the work where they left it. Mars is what we are going next for. The rest of this orrery is the syllabus.
