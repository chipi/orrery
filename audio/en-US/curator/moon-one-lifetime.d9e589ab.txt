On December 17th, 1903, on a beach at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, two bicycle mechanics from Ohio flew the first powered airplane. The flight lasted twelve seconds. The aircraft travelled one hundred and twenty feet. It was the first time in human history that a heavier-than-air machine, with a person aboard, lifted itself off the ground under its own power. Sixty-five years and seven months later — on July 20th, 1969 — Neil Armstrong stepped off the ladder of the Apollo 11 lunar module and put his boot down on the surface of the Moon. A person who, as a child, watched Orville Wright fly at Kitty Hawk was alive — and not even old — to watch Armstrong step onto another world. One human lifetime. From sand dunes to lunar regolith. What it took to get there is on the map in front of you. Twelve crewed missions. Six landings. Twenty-four astronauts who circled the Moon, twelve of whom walked on it. A budget that consumed four percent of the United States federal expenditure at its peak. Four hundred thousand engineers, technicians, and scientists. Three crewmen — Grissom, White, and Chaffee — who never came home, killed by a fire on the launch pad before any of the rest of the program could fly. What Apollo left behind on Earth is harder to see, but it is everywhere. The integrated-circuit industry was a curiosity in 1961 when Kennedy announced the program — a paying customer for half the world's supply of microchips by the time the Eagle landed eight years later. Mission control software became the ancestor of every flight management system that flies a commercial airliner today. Materials, life support, kidney dialysis, freeze-drying, satellite communications, the cameras in your phone — every one of those technologies has a thread that runs through Houston. Look at the map. Six landing sites on the near side. Zero on the far side. Zero at the poles. Fifty-two years since the last footstep — until Artemis. Within one human lifetime, we went there. The question now — looking at the map and the years and the gap — is what the next lifetime does with what the last one taught.
