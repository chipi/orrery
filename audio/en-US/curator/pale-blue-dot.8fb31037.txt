On February 14th, 1990, the Voyager 1 spacecraft was four billion miles from Earth. Almost six light-hours away. Its mission to the outer planets was complete. It was on its way out of the solar system, and it would never come back. The team at NASA sent a final command. Turn around. Look back at where you came from. Take a photograph. The image came back as a single point of light against the empty dark — less than one pixel across — suspended in a sunbeam the camera caught by accident. That dot is Earth. Carl Sagan wrote about it. Everyone you've ever heard of, he wrote — every saint and sinner, every founder of a religion, every couple in love, every parent waving goodbye to a child going to war — lived their entire life on that dot. Every aggregate of joy and suffering. Every confident religion. Every emperor and peasant. Happened there. You are looking at it now. Not the photograph — but the same dot. In this orrery you can pull back, see Earth's orbit around the Sun, see the Sun's place in the system, see the system's place in the dark. The view is the same view Voyager had. Only the distance differs. Take the tour. See where we are. See what we've done from here.
