Cassini launched in October 1997. It took almost seven years to reach Saturn — flybys of Venus, Venus again, Earth, and Jupiter built up the energy needed for the outer-system trip. It arrived in July 2004. Cassini spent the next thirteen years in orbit around Saturn. It dropped the Huygens probe onto Titan in January 2005 — the first soft landing on a moon of another planet. It discovered that Enceladus, Saturn's small icy moon, shoots geysers of water vapor from its south pole. Underneath that ice is a liquid ocean. Possibly with life-supporting chemistry. By 2017, Cassini was running low on fuel. And the mission team faced a problem: if they let the spacecraft drift, it might eventually crash into Enceladus or Titan — and contaminate worlds that, by then, were leading candidates for finding life beyond Earth. So they planned the Grand Finale. Twenty-two orbits, each one threading the gap between Saturn's cloud tops and the inner edge of its rings — a corridor about two thousand four hundred kilometers wide. No spacecraft had ever flown through it. Each orbit took the spacecraft closer to the planet's surface than the last. On September 15th, 2017, Cassini fired its thrusters one final time to hold its antenna toward Earth, and dove into Saturn's upper atmosphere. It transmitted live data — atmospheric composition, magnetic field, radio signatures of dust — until the radio link broke. Then it disintegrated. No spacecraft has been at Saturn since. The next planned arrival, Dragonfly to Titan, will launch in 2028. Cassini was twenty years between its launch and its end. Most of what you see on Saturn in this orrery — the rings, the moons, the planet's interior structure — is what Cassini learned.
