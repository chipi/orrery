Click Jupiter. Find the Great Red Spot — the oval, roughly the size of Earth, in the southern hemisphere. Now stop and consider what you're looking at. That is a single storm system. It has been there, continuously, for at least three hundred and fifty years. Astronomers first noted it through telescopes in 1665. It was a storm then. It is a storm now. Whatever combination of fluid dynamics, atmospheric chemistry, and Jupiter's rapid rotation sustains it has been keeping it spinning continuously since before the discovery of Newton's laws of motion. Jupiter rotates once every nine hours and fifty-five minutes. Faster than any other planet in the Solar System. That rotation drives winds in the Great Red Spot that reach four hundred and thirty kilometers per hour at its outer rim. Triple the strongest Earth hurricane. The spot has been shrinking. In 1879 it was forty thousand kilometers across. Today it is around fifteen thousand. Whether it eventually closes — or whether something keeps reseeding it out of the bands of upwelling gas around it — is one of the open questions Juno was sent to answer. Juno arrived in 2016. Its orbit takes it over Jupiter's poles — a polar orbit, a frame no previous Jupiter mission used — so it can map the planet's magnetic field, atmospheric chemistry, and the interior structure below the cloud tops. Photographs of the Great Red Spot from Juno are some of the most detailed ever taken from any spacecraft. Earth's worst hurricane lasts a few weeks. Jupiter has had the same hurricane for three and a half centuries. Look at it. Then notice the scale bar. Then breathe.
