Look at the diagram. At the top, far above Earth's orbit, is the James Webb Space Telescope . Not in orbit around Earth. Around a point in space that doesn't exist in any material sense. The second Sun-Earth Lagrange point — L2. Imagine a line from the Sun, through Earth, extending outward. One and a half million kilometers past Earth, in that exact direction, the combined gravity of the Sun and the Earth, plus the centripetal force of orbiting the Sun at Earth's pace, all balance out. A spacecraft placed there moves at exactly the same angular speed as Earth — completes one orbit per year — without burning fuel to hold station. But L2 is an unstable saddle point. Drift left or right, and gravity pulls you further away. So JWST doesn't sit at L2 — it orbits around L2, in a slow loop that takes about six months per revolution. A halo orbit. It never crosses the L2 point exactly; it traces a wide ellipse around it. Why L2 specifically? Two reasons. First — Earth sits between JWST and the Sun, almost continuously. The telescope's enormous sunshield, the size of a tennis court, permanently blocks sunlight and the heat radiating from the Earth and the Moon. JWST operates at forty Kelvin — about minus two hundred and thirty Celsius — because the heat-shield geometry never lets the cold side warm up. Second — by orbiting the Sun at Earth's pace, JWST stays close enough for high-bandwidth radio links, but far enough that the Earth's thermal emissions don't blind its infrared detectors. Hubble lives in low Earth orbit, five hundred fifty kilometers up, because it observes in visible and ultraviolet light. JWST lives at L2, one and a half million kilometers up, because it observes in the infrared. Different problem. Different orbit. Same universe.
