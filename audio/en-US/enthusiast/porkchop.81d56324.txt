This chart is called a porkchop plot. It looks like a folded pork chop. It is the most efficient way ever invented to argue with the solar system about when to launch. Every contour line is a constant amount of energy required to go from Earth to Mars. The horizontal axis is the launch date — when you leave. The vertical axis is the arrival date — when you get there. At the bottom of the C, energy is lowest. That's the cheap window. The further out you push from the bottom of the C, the more fuel you need. The more fuel you need, the heavier the rocket. The heavier the rocket, the more expensive everything else gets. Mars's cheap window opens once every twenty-six months . Twenty-six months because that's how long it takes for Earth — which orbits the Sun once a year — and Mars — which orbits once every one point eight eight years — to line up in the geometry where a transfer ellipse from one to the other is shortest. Look at the chart. Trace the bottom of the C. Notice how it bends. The cheapest day to leave isn't a single instant — it's a window two or three weeks wide. Now look at the next opening, twenty-six months later. The C-shape is in the same place, but the energy values are different. Because Mars's orbit isn't a perfect circle. Some launch windows are cheap. Some are very cheap. Mariner 4 in 1964. Viking in 1975. Pathfinder in 1996. Curiosity in 2011. Perseverance in 2020. Every Mars launch in history sits on the bottom of one of these C-shapes. The solar system doesn't care what you can build. It cares what date your launch pad is open. The porkchop plot tells you which dates the solar system has agreed to.
