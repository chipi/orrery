Click Saturn. Look at the rings. Now think about the numbers. Saturn itself is about one hundred and twenty thousand kilometers across. The main ring system stretches another two hundred and eighty thousand kilometers beyond the planet's edge. And those rings are, in places, only ten meters thick. Ten meters. A two-story building. A disc the diameter of nearly the distance from the Earth to the Moon, and it's the depth of a small house. The rings are not solid. They are billions of pieces of water ice, most of them centimeter-sized, some meter-sized, a few house-sized. Each piece is on its own Keplerian orbit. Pieces near the inner edge orbit Saturn in about five hours. Pieces near the outer edge take fourteen. And then there are the gaps. The Cassini Division — three thousand kilometers of nearly empty space between the A ring and the B ring — was carved by the gravity of Mimas, one of Saturn's small inner moons. Mimas is fifty thousand kilometers away. And yet, by orbital resonance — its two-to-one gravitational pull on any particle that tries to settle in that band — it has swept the Cassini Division clear. In September 2017, the Cassini spacecraft — having spent thirteen years in orbit around Saturn — was deliberately plunged into the planet's atmosphere. Its final twenty-two orbits, called the Grand Finale, took it weaving between the planet and the inner edge of the rings, photographing them from inside the gap. Those photographs are what you see when you look at the rings on this screen. Two hundred and eighty thousand kilometers wide. Ten meters thick. Read that again and let the ratio settle.
