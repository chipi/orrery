Look at the back of the International Space Station — the Russian end. The module furthest back, where the Soyuz and Progress vehicles dock, is called Zarya . In Russian, that means "sunrise." Zarya was the first piece of the ISS ever launched. November 20th, 1998. Proton-K rocket out of Baikonur. For the first two weeks, Zarya was just a single Russian-built canister in orbit, alone, providing its own power and attitude control while it waited. But Zarya isn't really a Russian module. It is technically called a Functional Cargo Block — F G B — a design built by Khrunichev, the same Moscow-based design bureau that built the Salyut and Mir space stations. And Zarya is owned by the United States. NASA paid for it — about two hundred and fifty million dollars in 1995 — because the original American design for the first ISS module ran late, and NASA needed a stand-in to keep the assembly schedule on track. Russian-built, American-owned, launched into a partnership between programs that, six years earlier, had been on opposite sides of the Cold War. It is hard to overstate what that arrangement looked like at the time. Two weeks after Zarya reached orbit, Endeavour launched on STS-88 carrying Unity — Node 1 — the first American-built module. On December 6th, 1998, the crew of Endeavour grabbed Zarya with the Shuttle's robotic arm and joined it to Unity. That moment — Zarya meeting Unity — is when the International Space Station went from "two separate satellites" to "a station." Over the next twelve and a half years, twenty more pressurized modules joined them, one Shuttle flight at a time. All of it built outward, in space, from the original Russian-American handshake at the back end of the station.
