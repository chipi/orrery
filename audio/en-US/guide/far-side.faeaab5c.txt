The Moon does something strange. It rotates on its axis at exactly the same rate that it orbits the Earth. That means one side of the Moon always faces us. The other side — the far side — is permanently turned away. No human eye saw the far side of the Moon until October 7th, 1959. Luna 3, a Soviet probe, returned the first photographs. They were grainy. Low contrast. And they showed something nobody had predicted: the far side looks different . Fewer of the dark plains — the maria — that dominate the near side. More cratered. Older. Rougher. No one has been there. Twelve people have stood on the Moon. All twelve stood on the near side. You can rotate this map and see the far side, but until the late 2010s, almost no spacecraft had even landed there. Then, in January 2019, Chang'e 4 made the first soft landing on the far side, inside the Von Kármán crater. To do this, China first had to launch a separate spacecraft — Queqiao , the magpie bridge — into a halo orbit around the Earth-Moon L2 Lagrange point beyond the Moon. Queqiao is the relay. Without it, a lander on the far side has no way to talk to Earth. The Moon is in the way. The Yutu-2 rover has been driving inside Von Kármán crater since that landing. It moves slowly. Sleeps through the fourteen-day lunar night. Wakes to study the regolith. Reports home through the magpie bridge. Most of the far side is still untouched. When the next crewed mission goes to the Moon, it will probably land on the near side — Artemis has chosen the south pole. Sometime after that, somebody will set foot on the far side for the first time. Imagine the photograph they'll send back. Look at this map and pick the spot.
