The view in front of you is Earth, with everything humans have placed in its orbital neighborhood. Each dot is a real spacecraft. Each label has a builder, a launch date, an operator, and a purpose. Look at the bottom of the diagram. Down close to the surface — about four hundred kilometers up — is the International Space Station, and slightly higher, Tiangong. Both stations are continuously crewed. Right now, as you listen, there are people living up there. Look slightly higher. Five hundred fifty kilometers up is Hubble. Hubble has been watching the universe since 1990, was serviced five times by Shuttle crews, and is still returning science. Higher still — around twenty thousand kilometers — are the navigation constellations : GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou. Without these, the maps on your phone would not work. Higher still — thirty-six thousand kilometers — is geostationary orbit . At that altitude, a satellite's orbital period is exactly twenty-four hours. Match the rotation of the Earth and the satellite appears to hang in one spot above the surface forever. Every weather satellite, every direct-broadcast television satellite, lives here. Higher still — much higher — at about one and a half million kilometers from Earth — is the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point , the place where the gravity of the Sun and the gravity of the Earth balance. The James Webb Space Telescope orbits there. So does the European Space Agency's Gaia, which is mapping a billion stars. The scale on this map is logarithmic . It has to be. Hubble at five hundred kilometers and JWST at one million five hundred thousand kilometers would, on a linear scale, be separated by a factor of three thousand seven hundred and fifty. Hubble would be invisible. The log scale gives every regime — low Earth orbit, medium Earth orbit, geostationary, beyond — its own visible band. Click any spacecraft. The panel opens with its launch story, its purpose, and its operator. Click ISS or Tiangong, and the panel offers a link to the dedicated station explorer — where every module of the station is pickable, rotatable, and attributable to the agency that built it. Earth's orbit has gotten crowded over fifty years. Most of what's up there does work you depend on without knowing. Look at the labels. Read the dates. See how recently most of this arrived.
