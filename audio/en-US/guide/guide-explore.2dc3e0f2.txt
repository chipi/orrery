What you see on screen is the solar system. The Sun at the center. Eight planets in their orbits. Pluto and Ceres if you ask for them. The positions you see are the actual positions today — Keplerian elements computed for the current Julian date. Drag to rotate the view. Scroll to zoom in and out. Click any planet, and the right panel opens with its physical data, orbit parameters, and gallery. Look at the orbit lines. Mercury makes one trip every eighty-eight Earth days. Mars takes one point eight eight Earth years. Saturn takes twenty-nine point five Earth years. Neptune takes one hundred sixty-five. Neptune has been in its current position, relative to the Sun, exactly once since its discovery in 1846. It just completed its first full orbit, by our calendar, in 2011. Watch the planets move. The time slider at the bottom lets you speed up the simulation — one day per second, ten days, a hundred. Pause it on any date and read off the positions. Notice the scale. The orbit radii in this view are compressed on a logarithmic scale, because if we drew them to true scale you wouldn't be able to see anything past Jupiter without scrolling for half an hour. The orbital velocities are real, though. Earth moves at thirty kilometers per second relative to the Sun. You don't feel it because you're moving with it. Try the Science Lens toggle at the top right. It overlays additional physical data — orbital eccentricity, axial tilt, the inclination of each orbital plane to Earth's. Hover any planet with the lens on and you'll see the velocity number change as it moves along its ellipse — faster at perihelion, slower at aphelion. Vis-viva, in action. Click the Sun. Read the panel. Then click any planet, and trace its path with your eye. Every other screen in this Orrery — the mission maps, the station explorers, the fleet — was made to explain a single object that you can see right now, here, moving with the others.
