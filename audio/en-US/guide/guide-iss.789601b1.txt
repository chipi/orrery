What you see rotating in the center of your screen is the International Space Station — to scale, every truss, every solar array, every pressurized module. At its full length, ISS is one hundred nine meters end to end. A football pitch. Drag to rotate. Each module is clickable. Click any one, and the panel opens with its builder, its launch vehicle, its launch date, and its function. Start at the Russian end. Zarya — the Functional Cargo Block — was the first module of the station. Built by Khrunichev for Russia, paid for by NASA, launched on a Proton-K rocket on November 20th, 1998. Zarya provided power, propulsion, and attitude control for the first two years, while the rest of the station was being assembled around it. Move forward in time. Unity — also called Node 1 — was the second module. Launched on Endeavour on December 4th, 1998. Two weeks later, Endeavour's crew connected Zarya to Unity in the first on-orbit assembly of the station. Click Destiny. Destiny is the United States laboratory module. Launched on STS-98 in 2001, it has been the primary American science workspace for over twenty years. Click Columbus. That is the European laboratory module, launched by Atlantis in 2008. Click Kibo. That is the Japanese experiment module — the largest single ISS module, launched in three flights between 2008 and 2009. Look at the solar arrays. Eight of them — the original four installed between 2000 and 2009, four more rolled out between 2021 and 2023. Together they produce about two hundred forty kilowatts of electrical power — enough for a hundred and twenty average American homes. Look at the trusses connecting it all. The integrated truss structure is the station's skeleton. It was assembled segment by segment by Shuttle crews between 2000 and 2011. No spacecraft has carried any of those segments since the Shuttle's retirement. If a new truss segment were needed today, no current launch vehicle could carry it. Eighteen pressurized modules. Eighteen builders. Eighteen launch dates between 1998 and 2021. Two hundred and seventy people from twenty-six countries have lived inside. Click any module. Read who made it, when, and what it does.
