This is the Mission Configurator. You pick a destination — Mars, Venus, Mercury, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, Pluto, or the asteroid Ceres. The screen draws a chart called a porkchop plot , and the chart tells you every possible launch date for that destination, paired with every possible arrival date, with a cost number for each combination. Look at the chart. The horizontal axis is the launch date. The vertical axis is the arrival date. The colored contour lines are constant energy — each one represents a trajectory that costs the same amount of fuel. At the bottom of the C-shape, the contours are cheapest. That's where the spacecraft want to leave. Click anywhere on the chart. The right panel tells you exactly what that launch-and-arrival combination would mean — departure date, arrival date, transit time, departure C three in kilometers squared per second squared, arrival V infinity in kilometers per second. Notice how the cheap window moves. Mars's cheap window appears once every twenty-six months — every time Earth and Mars line up in the right geometry. Drag the time slider above the chart to walk forward five or ten years. You'll see the C-shape recur — same shape, different dates, slightly different energy values because Mars's orbit isn't a perfect circle. Try a different destination. Switch to Jupiter. Notice that the cheap window is rarer — about thirteen months apart — and the transit time is much longer, often years. Switch to Mercury. Notice that the chart looks completely different — Mercury is close, but it moves so fast around the Sun that catching it is its own engineering problem. When you find a point on the chart you like, click the Fly this mission button at the bottom of the right panel. The configurator hands you off to the Fly screen, where you watch the trajectory you just picked play out in heliocentric coordinates against the moving planets. Real missions plan exactly this way. Every Mars rover, every Venus probe, every Galileo and Cassini and Voyager — somebody ran a porkchop plot first, picked the cheap window in the bottom of the C, and the rocket left the launch pad on a date the solar system had agreed to. Pick a destination. Click the chart. Argue with the geometry.
