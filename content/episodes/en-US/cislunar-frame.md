<speak>
  Open the Fly screen and look at a Mars mission. <break time="500ms"/>
  Now look at a Moon mission. <break time="500ms"/>
  The two views look completely different — even though both spacecraft
  are doing the same thing: following an elliptical trajectory under
  gravity.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  The difference is the <emphasis level="moderate">reference frame.</emphasis>

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Mars missions are drawn <emphasis level="moderate">heliocentric.</emphasis>
  <break time="400ms"/>
  The Sun sits at the center of the view. <break time="400ms"/>
  Earth and Mars trace their orbits around it.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  The spacecraft's arc is a piece of an ellipse around the Sun, with
  perihelion at Earth's distance and aphelion out near Mars.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  This frame makes the geometry legible — you can see the spacecraft
  catching up to Mars as Mars itself moves.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Moon missions are drawn <emphasis level="moderate">cislunar.</emphasis>
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Earth sits at the center of the view. <break time="400ms"/>
  The Moon orbits around it. <break time="400ms"/>
  The spacecraft's arc — a translunar injection from low Earth orbit,
  cruise to lunar arrival, lunar orbit insertion — is drawn against
  Earth's reference frame, because everything important is happening in
  Earth's gravity well.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Drawn heliocentric, this trajectory would be a tiny squiggle next to
  Earth's orbit around the Sun. You'd see nothing useful.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Both views are correct. <break time="400ms"/>
  The spacecraft is doing the same thing in both — gravity does not change
  with your choice of frame.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  But what you can <emphasis level="moderate">see</emphasis> changes.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  A Mars mission needs heliocentric scale to be readable. <break time="300ms"/>
  A Moon mission needs Earth-centered scale to be readable.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Use the toolbar to switch frames on any mission. <break time="500ms"/>
  Watch what becomes visible in each. <break time="400ms"/>
  This is the same trick astronomers use when they switch between
  heliocentric, geocentric, and barycentric coordinates depending on
  what they want to compute.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Choose the frame the problem lives in.
</speak>
