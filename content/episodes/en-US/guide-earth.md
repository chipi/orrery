<speak>
  The view in front of you is Earth, with everything humans have placed in
  its orbital neighborhood. <break time="500ms"/>
  Each dot is a real spacecraft. <break time="400ms"/>
  Each label has a builder, a launch date, an operator, and a purpose.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Look at the bottom of the diagram. <break time="500ms"/>
  Down close to the surface — about four hundred kilometers up — is the
  International Space Station, and slightly higher, Tiangong.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Both stations are continuously crewed.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Right now, as you listen, there are people living up there.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Look slightly higher. <break time="500ms"/>
  Five hundred fifty kilometers up is Hubble. <break time="400ms"/>
  Hubble has been watching the universe since 1990, was serviced five times by
  Shuttle crews, and is still returning science.
  <break time="700ms"/>
  Higher still — around twenty thousand kilometers — are the
  <emphasis level="moderate">navigation constellations</emphasis>: GPS,
  Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Without these, the maps on your phone would not work.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Higher still — thirty-six thousand kilometers — is
  <emphasis level="moderate">geostationary orbit</emphasis>. <break time="400ms"/>
  At that altitude, a satellite's orbital period is exactly twenty-four hours.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Match the rotation of the Earth and the satellite appears to hang in one
  spot above the surface forever.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Every weather satellite, every direct-broadcast television satellite, lives
  here.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Higher still — much higher — at about one and a half million kilometers from
  Earth — is the second <emphasis level="moderate">Sun-Earth Lagrange point</emphasis>,
  the place where the gravity of the Sun and the gravity of the Earth balance.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The James Webb Space Telescope orbits there. <break time="400ms"/>
  So does the European Space Agency's Gaia, which is mapping a billion stars.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  The scale on this map is <emphasis level="moderate">logarithmic</emphasis>.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  It has to be. <break time="500ms"/>
  Hubble at five hundred kilometers and JWST at one million five hundred
  thousand kilometers would, on a linear scale, be separated by a factor of
  three thousand seven hundred and fifty. <break time="500ms"/>
  Hubble would be invisible. <break time="500ms"/>
  The log scale gives every regime — low Earth orbit, medium Earth orbit,
  geostationary, beyond — its own visible band.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Click any spacecraft. <break time="500ms"/>
  The panel opens with its launch story, its purpose, and its operator.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Click ISS or Tiangong, and the panel offers a link to the dedicated station
  explorer — where every module of the station is pickable, rotatable, and
  attributable to the agency that built it.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Earth's orbit has gotten crowded over fifty years. <break time="500ms"/>
  Most of what's up there does work you depend on without knowing.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Look at the labels. <break time="400ms"/> Read the dates. <break time="400ms"/>
  See how recently most of this arrived.
</speak>
