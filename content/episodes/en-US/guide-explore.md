<speak>
  What you see on screen is the solar system. <break time="500ms"/>
  The Sun at the center. <break time="300ms"/> Eight planets in their orbits.
  <break time="300ms"/> Pluto and Ceres if you ask for them.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The positions you see are the <emphasis level="moderate">actual positions today</emphasis> —
  Keplerian elements computed for the current Julian date.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Drag to rotate the view. <break time="400ms"/>
  Scroll to zoom in and out. <break time="400ms"/>
  Click any planet, and the right panel opens with its physical data, orbit
  parameters, and gallery.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Look at the orbit lines. <break time="500ms"/>
  Mercury makes one trip every eighty-eight Earth days. <break time="400ms"/>
  Mars takes one point eight eight Earth years.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Saturn takes <emphasis level="moderate">twenty-nine point five</emphasis> Earth years.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Neptune takes one hundred sixty-five.
  <break time="700ms"/>
  Neptune has been in its current position, relative to the Sun, exactly once
  since its discovery in 1846. <break time="400ms"/>
  It just completed its first full orbit, by our calendar, in 2011.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Watch the planets move. <break time="500ms"/>
  The time slider at the bottom lets you speed up the simulation — one day per
  second, ten days, a hundred. <break time="500ms"/>
  Pause it on any date and read off the positions.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Notice the scale. <break time="500ms"/>
  The orbit radii in this view are <emphasis level="moderate">compressed</emphasis>
  on a logarithmic scale, because if we drew them to true scale you wouldn't be
  able to see anything past Jupiter without scrolling for half an hour.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The orbital <emphasis level="moderate">velocities</emphasis> are real, though.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Earth moves at thirty kilometers per second relative to the Sun.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  You don't feel it because you're moving with it.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Try the Science Lens toggle at the top right. <break time="500ms"/>
  It overlays additional physical data — orbital eccentricity, axial tilt, the
  inclination of each orbital plane to Earth's.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Hover any planet with the lens on and you'll see the velocity number change
  as it moves along its ellipse — faster at perihelion, slower at aphelion.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Vis-viva, in action.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Click the Sun. <break time="500ms"/>
  Read the panel. <break time="500ms"/>
  Then click any planet, and trace its path with your eye.
  <break time="700ms"/>
  Every other screen in this Orrery — the mission maps, the station explorers,
  the fleet — was made to explain a single object that you can see right now,
  here, moving with the others.
</speak>
