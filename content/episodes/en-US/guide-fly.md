<speak>
  This view is called a <emphasis level="moderate">mission arc</emphasis>.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  You picked a mission — say, the transfer from Earth to Mars — and the screen
  shows you the actual trajectory the spacecraft will fly, in heliocentric
  coordinates, against the orbits of the planets.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Look at the arc. <break time="500ms"/>
  It is not a straight line. <break time="400ms"/>
  It cannot be a straight line. <break time="500ms"/>
  When the spacecraft leaves Earth, it leaves with Earth's velocity around
  the Sun — about thirty kilometers per second — plus whatever the rocket
  adds on top.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  That total velocity puts the spacecraft on a <emphasis level="moderate">Keplerian
  ellipse</emphasis> around the Sun. <break time="400ms"/>
  The ellipse's perihelion sits at Earth's distance. <break time="400ms"/>
  Its aphelion reaches out to wherever it's going — Mars in this case.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Press play. <break time="500ms"/>
  Watch the spacecraft slide along the arc. <break time="400ms"/>
  Watch Earth and Mars move at the same time.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Notice how the spacecraft's arrival has to be timed against Mars's own motion —
  Mars is moving while you're flying.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  If you leave a month too late, Mars will have moved on, and your arc — which
  is set by physics, not by you — will arrive at empty space.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  This is what the <emphasis level="moderate">porkchop plot</emphasis> on the
  Plan screen is computing. <break time="500ms"/>
  For every possible launch date and arrival date, what does the arc cost in
  energy, and does the geometry line up?

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Some missions add a <emphasis level="moderate">cislunar view</emphasis> —
  switchable from the toolbar. <break time="500ms"/>
  Cislunar is Earth-centered, not Sun-centered. <break time="400ms"/>
  It shows you what an Apollo crew or an Artemis crew sees: Earth in the
  middle, the Moon orbiting around, the spacecraft riding a trans-lunar
  injection arc to lunar orbit and back.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Heliocentric arcs and cislunar arcs are two different geometries — same
  spacecraft, different reference frame. <break time="400ms"/>
  Use whichever makes the mission make sense.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Below the view, the timeline lists the mission's phases — parking orbit,
  trans-Mars injection, cruise, mid-course corrections, arrival. <break time="400ms"/>
  Click any phase chip. <break time="400ms"/>
  The animation jumps to that moment in the mission. <break time="500ms"/>
  Pause it. <break time="400ms"/> Read the numbers. <break time="500ms"/>
  Restart and watch the next phase begin.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Every mission you can fly here actually flew, or is scheduled to fly.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The arc is real. <break time="400ms"/>
  The clock is real. <break time="400ms"/>
  Only the speed has been adjusted, so a seven-month transfer fits on your
  screen in seventy seconds.
</speak>
