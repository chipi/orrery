<speak>
  What you see rotating in the center of your screen is the International
  Space Station — to scale, every truss, every solar array, every
  pressurized module.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  At its full length, ISS is one hundred nine meters end to end. <break time="400ms"/>
  A football pitch.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Drag to rotate. <break time="400ms"/>
  Each module is clickable. <break time="500ms"/>
  Click any one, and the panel opens with its builder, its launch vehicle,
  its launch date, and its function.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Start at the Russian end. <break time="500ms"/>
  Zarya — the Functional Cargo Block — was the first module of the station.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  Built by Khrunichev for Russia, paid for by NASA, launched on a Proton-K
  rocket on November 20th, 1998.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Zarya provided power, propulsion, and attitude control for the first two
  years, while the rest of the station was being assembled around it.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Move forward in time. <break time="500ms"/>
  Unity — also called Node 1 — was the second module. Launched on Endeavour
  on December 4th, 1998. <break time="400ms"/>
  Two weeks later, Endeavour's crew connected Zarya to Unity in the first
  on-orbit assembly of the station.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Click Destiny. <break time="500ms"/>
  Destiny is the United States laboratory module. <break time="400ms"/>
  Launched on STS-98 in 2001, it has been the primary American science
  workspace for over twenty years.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Click Columbus. <break time="400ms"/>
  That is the European laboratory module, launched by Atlantis in 2008.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Click Kibo. <break time="400ms"/>
  That is the Japanese experiment module — the largest single ISS module,
  launched in three flights between 2008 and 2009.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Look at the solar arrays. <break time="500ms"/>
  Eight of them — the original four installed between 2000 and 2009, four more
  rolled out between 2021 and 2023.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Together they produce about <emphasis level="moderate">two hundred forty
  kilowatts</emphasis> of electrical power — enough for a hundred and twenty
  average American homes.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Look at the trusses connecting it all. <break time="500ms"/>
  The integrated truss structure is the station's skeleton. <break time="400ms"/>
  It was assembled segment by segment by Shuttle crews between 2000 and 2011.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  No spacecraft has carried any of those segments since the Shuttle's
  retirement. <break time="400ms"/>
  If a new truss segment were needed today, no current launch vehicle could
  carry it.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Eighteen pressurized modules. <break time="200ms"/>
  Eighteen builders. <break time="200ms"/>
  Eighteen launch dates between 1998 and 2021.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Two hundred and seventy people from twenty-six countries have lived inside.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Click any module. <break time="400ms"/>
  Read who made it, when, and what it does.
</speak>
