<speak>
  This is the Mission Configurator. <break time="500ms"/>
  You pick a destination — Mars, Venus, Mercury, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus,
  Neptune, Pluto, or the asteroid Ceres. <break time="500ms"/>
  The screen draws a chart called a
  <emphasis level="moderate">porkchop plot</emphasis>, and the chart tells you
  every possible launch date for that destination, paired with every possible
  arrival date, with a cost number for each combination.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Look at the chart. <break time="500ms"/>
  The horizontal axis is the launch date. <break time="400ms"/>
  The vertical axis is the arrival date.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The colored contour lines are constant energy — each one represents a
  trajectory that costs the same amount of fuel.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  At the bottom of the C-shape, the contours are cheapest.
  <break time="400ms"/> That's where the spacecraft want to leave.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Click anywhere on the chart. <break time="500ms"/>
  The right panel tells you exactly what that launch-and-arrival combination
  would mean — departure date, arrival date, transit time, departure
  <emphasis level="moderate">C three</emphasis> in kilometers squared per
  second squared, arrival
  <emphasis level="moderate">V infinity</emphasis> in kilometers per second.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Notice how the cheap window moves. <break time="500ms"/>
  Mars's cheap window appears once every twenty-six months — every time Earth
  and Mars line up in the right geometry.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Drag the time slider above the chart to walk forward five or ten years.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  You'll see the C-shape recur — same shape, different dates, slightly
  different energy values because Mars's orbit isn't a perfect circle.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Try a different destination. <break time="500ms"/>
  Switch to Jupiter. <break time="400ms"/>
  Notice that the cheap window is rarer — about thirteen months apart —
  and the transit time is much longer, often years.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Switch to Mercury. <break time="400ms"/>
  Notice that the chart looks completely different — Mercury is close, but
  it moves so fast around the Sun that catching it is its own engineering
  problem.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  When you find a point on the chart you like, click the
  <emphasis level="moderate">Fly this mission</emphasis> button at the bottom
  of the right panel. <break time="500ms"/>
  The configurator hands you off to the Fly screen, where you watch the
  trajectory you just picked play out in heliocentric coordinates against the
  moving planets.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Real missions plan exactly this way. <break time="500ms"/>
  Every Mars rover, every Venus probe, every Galileo and Cassini and
  Voyager — somebody ran a porkchop plot first, picked the cheap window
  in the bottom of the C, and the rocket left the launch pad on a date
  the solar system had agreed to.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Pick a destination. <break time="500ms"/>
  Click the chart. <break time="500ms"/>
  Argue with the geometry.
</speak>
