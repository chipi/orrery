<speak>
  <prosody rate="90%" pitch="-1st">
    The question is often phrased: <break time="400ms"/>
    <emphasis level="moderate">why Mars?</emphasis>
    <break time="700ms"/>
    Why send people across one hundred and forty million kilometers of empty
    sunshine, to a planet that has no breathable air, no liquid water, no
    magnetic field to shield them from cosmic rays, no fast way home?
  </prosody>

  <break time="1000ms"/>

  <prosody rate="88%" pitch="-1st">
    Mars is not where we are going next. <break time="600ms"/>
    Mars is what we are going next <emphasis level="strong">for</emphasis>.
  </prosody>

  <break time="1100ms"/>

  <prosody rate="88%" pitch="-1st">
    Look at the screens in this orrery. <break time="500ms"/>
    Every rover, every orbiter, every lander placed on the Moon since 2007
    — Chang'e, Chandrayaan, SLIM, Artemis flight planning —
    <break time="500ms"/>
    is, in some sense, a rehearsal.
    <break time="500ms"/>
    Every module of the International Space Station, every Tiangong segment,
    every long-duration crew that has lived continuously in microgravity
    for the past quarter-century —
    <break time="500ms"/>
    is a rehearsal.
    <break time="500ms"/>
    Every reusable booster lifting payload to orbit at a tenth of the cost
    it took twenty years ago —
    <break time="500ms"/>
    is a rehearsal.
  </prosody>

  <break time="1100ms"/>

  <prosody rate="86%" pitch="-1st">
    Mars is the destination that organizes the others.
    <break time="500ms"/>
    A Moon base teaches us to live off-world without going so far that a
    week's resupply is impossible. <break time="500ms"/>
    A continuous space station teaches us what a year of microgravity does
    to muscle, bone, vision, and mind. <break time="500ms"/>
    A rover landed by a robotic teleoperator teaches us autonomy at the
    distance where conversation cannot.
  </prosody>

  <break time="1200ms"/>

  <prosody rate="84%" pitch="-1st">
    The argument for Mars is not the argument that it is easy. <break time="400ms"/>
    It is not. <break time="500ms"/>
    The argument is that the questions Mars asks have no other answer.
    <break time="600ms"/>
    Is life common in the universe? <break time="400ms"/>
    Was Mars once Earth-like, and what did it lose, and how? <break time="500ms"/>
    Can a multi-celled biosphere migrate? <break time="500ms"/>
    Can a civilization?
  </prosody>

  <break time="1200ms"/>

  <prosody rate="86%" pitch="-1st">
    Look at this map. <break time="500ms"/>
    Look at the rovers already on it — <break time="400ms"/>
    silent, persistent, slowly tracing across the planet on errands set by
    teams two hundred million kilometers away.
    <break time="700ms"/>
    Every one of them is a postcard sent ahead.
    <break time="500ms"/>
    They are waiting for whoever comes to pick up the work where they left it.
  </prosody>

  <break time="1100ms"/>

  <prosody rate="84%" pitch="-1st">
    Mars is what we are going next for. <break time="500ms"/>
    The rest of this orrery is the syllabus.
  </prosody>
</speak>
