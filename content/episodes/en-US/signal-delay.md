<speak>
  When Curiosity radios home from Mars, the signal travels in a straight line at the speed of light.
  <break time="600ms"/>

  And it still takes anywhere from <emphasis level="moderate">three minutes</emphasis> to
  <emphasis level="moderate">twenty-two minutes</emphasis> to arrive.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Three minutes when Mars and Earth are on the same side of the Sun, closest together.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Twenty-two minutes when Mars is on the far side of the Sun, on the other side of the inner system.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The radio isn't slow. <break time="400ms"/> The universe is large.

  <break time="700ms"/>

  This is why <emphasis level="strong">no human on Earth has ever driven Curiosity in real time.</emphasis>
  <break time="500ms"/>
  A joystick command — "turn left" — would arrive on Mars somewhere between three and twenty-two
  minutes after you sent it. <break time="400ms"/> By then the rover would already be eight meters
  past the rock you were trying to avoid.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  So Curiosity doesn't have a driver. <break time="400ms"/> It has a planner.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Each Martian day — each <emphasis level="moderate">sol</emphasis> — the team at JPL sends a
  sequence of commands the rover executes <emphasis level="strong">autonomously</emphasis>.
  Drive forward twelve meters. Stop. Take seven photos. Choose the safest of three onward paths.

  <break time="600ms"/>

  The rover sleeps. <break time="300ms"/> Earth sleeps. <break time="300ms"/> Mars rotates.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  And when both planets wake the next sol, fourteen minutes apart, the next set of commands flies
  through the dark to the rover that's already been working in the dark for sixteen hours.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Fourteen minutes. <break time="500ms"/> Not slow. <break time="300ms"/> Just far.
</speak>
