<speak>
  In the late 1960s, a young engineer at JPL named Gary Flandro was running
  the math on how planetary positions repeat.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  He noticed that in the late 1970s, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune
  would all sit on the same side of the Sun, roughly in line.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  A single spacecraft could use Jupiter's gravity to slingshot to Saturn,
  Saturn's to slingshot to Uranus, Uranus's to reach Neptune.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  The alignment comes around once every one hundred and seventy-five years.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The previous one had been during Thomas Jefferson's presidency.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  The next one will be in the twenty-second century.
  <break time="700ms"/>
  Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 launched in 1977 to catch it.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Voyager 2 flew past Jupiter in 1979, Saturn in 1981, Uranus in 1986,
  and Neptune in 1989. <break time="500ms"/>
  It is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus or Neptune at all.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Voyager 1 took a different option at Saturn — it traded Uranus and
  Neptune for a close look at Titan's atmosphere, then turned upward out
  of the ecliptic plane.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Both spacecraft are still operating. <break time="500ms"/>
  Voyager 1 is the most distant human-made object — currently about
  twenty-four billion kilometers from the Sun. <break time="400ms"/>
  It crossed into interstellar space in 2012.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Voyager 2 followed in 2018, on a different trajectory through the
  heliopause.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Each spacecraft carries the Golden Record — a phonograph disc with
  recordings of natural sounds, music from twenty-seven cultures, and
  greetings in fifty-five languages. <break time="500ms"/>
  Carl Sagan led the team that chose what to put on it.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The cover has instructions, in pulsar-position notation and hydrogen-atom
  diagrams, for any future intelligence that might find it.

  <break time="800ms"/>

  The spacecraft will keep going for tens of thousands of years.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  They will outlive the agencies that built them, the countries that funded
  them, and possibly the species that listened to the records.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  Most of the missions you will see in this catalogue were possible because
  Voyager went first.
</speak>
