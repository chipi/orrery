<speak>
  Look at the back of the International Space Station — the Russian end.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  The module furthest back, where the Soyuz and Progress vehicles dock,
  is called <emphasis level="moderate">Zarya</emphasis>.
  <break time="400ms"/>
  In Russian, that means "sunrise."

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Zarya was the first piece of the ISS ever launched. <break time="500ms"/>
  November 20th, 1998. <break time="400ms"/>
  Proton-K rocket out of Baikonur.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  For the first two weeks, Zarya was just a single Russian-built canister
  in orbit, alone, providing its own power and attitude control while it
  waited.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  But Zarya isn't really a Russian module. <break time="500ms"/>
  It is technically called a <emphasis level="moderate">Functional Cargo
  Block</emphasis> — F G B — a design built by Khrunichev, the same
  Moscow-based design bureau that built the Salyut and Mir space stations.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  And Zarya is owned by the United States.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  NASA paid for it — about two hundred and fifty million dollars in 1995 —
  because the original American design for the first ISS module ran late,
  and NASA needed a stand-in to keep the assembly schedule on track.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Russian-built, American-owned, launched into a partnership between
  programs that, six years earlier, had been on opposite sides of the
  Cold War.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  It is hard to overstate what that arrangement looked like at the time.

  <break time="900ms"/>

  Two weeks after Zarya reached orbit, Endeavour launched on STS-88 carrying
  <emphasis level="moderate">Unity</emphasis> — Node 1 — the first
  American-built module.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  On December 6th, 1998, the crew of Endeavour grabbed Zarya with the
  Shuttle's robotic arm and joined it to Unity.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  That moment — Zarya meeting Unity — is when the International Space
  Station went from "two separate satellites" to "a station."

  <break time="800ms"/>

  Over the next twelve and a half years, twenty more pressurized modules
  joined them, one Shuttle flight at a time.
  <break time="500ms"/>
  All of it built outward, in space, from the original Russian-American
  handshake at the back end of the station.
</speak>
