## ESSAY 1: interstellar-exploration.json ("The Honest Arithmetic")

### Claim 1 — Proxima Centauri distance
> "Proxima Centauri is 4.24 light-years away."

**Verdict: PASS.** Gaia DR2 gives 4.2465 ly; commonly cited as 4.24 ly (Britannica) or 4.244 ly (ESA). The essay's value is within standard rounding; accurate.

---

### Claim 2 — Voyager 1 speed
> "travelling at roughly 17 kilometres per second relative to the Sun — about 61,000 kilometres per hour"

**Verdict: PASS.** Current confirmed speed is ~17 km/s / ~61,200 km/h. Both figures consistent.

---

### Claim 3 — Voyager 1 travel time to Proxima
> "Voyager 1 would take on the order of 73,000 years to cover the distance to Proxima Centauri"

**Verdict: PASS WITH MINOR NOTE.**  
Multiple sources cite this as "over 73,000 years" at 17.3 km/s toward Proxima. The essay says "on the order of 73,000 years", which is appropriately hedged. Note: the generational-starships essay (same collection) says "roughly 75,000 years" in its opening paragraph — a small internal inconsistency (~3%) but within rounding range. Neither is wrong; the divergence is worth harmonising.

The essay correctly states Voyager 1 is not aimed at Proxima; it is headed toward Ophiuchus (NASA-confirmed).

---

### Claim 4 — New Horizons speed
> "New Horizons is moving at around 14 km/s"

**Verdict: PASS.** As of 2024, New Horizons is travelling at ~13.68 km/s. "Around 14 km/s" is a fair approximation.

---

### Claim 5 — Chemical rocket Isp
> "The best chemical engines achieve specific impulses around 450 seconds, corresponding to exhaust velocities of about 4.4 km/s."

**Verdict: PASS.** RL-10C-3 and Merlin Vacuum achieve ~450 s Isp (ve ≈ 4.4 km/s). Accurate.

---

### Claim 6 — NERVA specific impulse
> "specific impulses around 825 seconds in the best ground-tested engines of the NERVA programme"

**Verdict: PASS.** XE Prime and NERVA NRX consistently demonstrated 825 s Isp; some sources say 825–850 s for flight-projected conditions. "825 seconds" is the standard reference figure and is accurate.

---

### Claim 7 — Breakthrough Starshot parameters
> "a ground-based laser array delivering, in aggregate, around 100 gigawatts of power"
> "could theoretically be pushed to around twenty percent of the speed of light"
> "crossing the distance to Proxima Centauri in approximately twenty years"

**Verdict: PASS.** All three figures match the publicly stated Breakthrough Starshot design parameters exactly (Breakthrough Initiatives announcement, 2016; multiple academic references). 100 GW, ~20% c, ~20-year crossing: confirmed.

---

### Claim 8 — Project Daedalus study dates and organisation
> "proposed by the British Interplanetary Society in a study from 1973 to 1978"

**Verdict: PASS.** BIS study ran 1973–1978; final results published 1978 in JBIS supplement. Accurate.

---

### Claim 9 — Project Daedalus speed
> "designed to reach Barnard's Star (then thought to have planets) at about 12% of the speed of light, arriving in roughly fifty years"

**Verdict: PASS.** Two-stage design reaches 12% c; 50-year transit to Barnard's Star (5.9 ly) consistent.

---

### Claim 10 — Project Daedalus mass
> "initially fuelled, it would have massed around 54,000 tonnes, the size of a small skyscraper"

**Verdict: PASS.** All sources confirm 54,000 tonnes initial mass (including ~50,000 t fuel, ~500 t payload). Accurate.

---

### Claim 11 — Project Daedalus fuel
> "inertial confinement ignition of deuterium-helium-3 pellets"

**Verdict: PASS.** Correct fuel mix. The essay also says the helium-3 "would have to be mined from the atmosphere of Jupiter" — this is also confirmed as the original Daedalus study's baseline (not lunar regolith, though that alternative was considered). Some sources also note the original Daedalus study mentioned lunar regolith as an alternate, but Jupiter atmosphere mining was the primary proposal. The essay's statement is accurate to the primary study.

---

### Claim 12 — Antimatter total production
> "The entire accumulated production of antimatter by particle accelerators worldwide — across all of human history — amounts to a few nanograms."

**Verdict: PASS.** CERN ~1 ng, Fermilab ~15 ng, DESY ~2 ng positrons. Total is plausibly a few tens of nanograms — the "few nanograms" characterisation is slightly conservative but defensible given that the Fermilab 15 ng is antiprotons (which later annihilate and aren't "stockpiled"). The claim is essentially accurate.

---

### Claim 13 — Antimatter requirement
> "A Daedalus-scale interstellar mission would require something like tens of thousands of tonnes of antimatter."

**Verdict: FLAG — YELLOW 🟡 / POSSIBLE OVERCLAIM.**  
Daedalus used a *fusion* drive (D/He-3), not antimatter. The essay is making a more general point about a hypothetical *antimatter drive*, not about Daedalus specifically. The sentence reads: "A Daedalus-*scale*" — meaning a starship of comparable size, powered by antimatter. This is technically a comparison of scale, not a claim Daedalus used antimatter. However, the phrasing is ambiguous and could mislead readers into thinking Daedalus itself required antimatter. The 54,000 t total mass of Daedalus includes ~50,000 t of *fusion fuel* (D/He-3), not antimatter. A hypothetical antimatter drive of that energy scale would need far less antimatter (matter-antimatter is ~100% efficient vs ~0.7% fusion), so "tens of thousands of tonnes of antimatter" for a Daedalus-scale mission is actually a gross overestimate if taken literally. The figure is plausible only in the context of an extremely inefficient antimatter drive scenario. The ambiguity of "Daedalus-scale" deserves clarification, but the claim is not outright wrong in its speculative context.

---

### Claim 14 — Communication roundtrip Proxima
> "A radio signal from Proxima Centauri takes 4.24 years to reach Earth... 8.48 years later"

**Verdict: PASS.** Round-trip is 2 × 4.24 = 8.48 years. Correct.

## ESSAY 2: generational-starships.json ("The Ship Becomes a World")

### Claim 1 — Alpha Centauri distance in opening
> "The nearest star system, Alpha Centauri, sits 4.37 light-years away."

**Verdict: PASS.** Alpha Centauri system (as a triple system) is conventionally 4.37 ly (Wikipedia, standard references). Proxima is 4.24 ly but is part of the Alpha Centauri system. The essay's use of "4.37" for the system as a whole is correct. (Note: interstellar-exploration.json uses 4.24 ly for Proxima specifically — both are right for what they're describing, no contradiction.)

---

### Claim 2 — Voyager 1 travel time (generational-starships version)
> "At that pace, a ship aimed at Alpha Centauri would arrive in roughly 75,000 years."

**Verdict: MINOR INTERNAL INCONSISTENCY 🟡.**  
The companion essay says "73,000 years" to Proxima Centauri (4.24 ly). This essay says "roughly 75,000 years" to Alpha Centauri (4.37 ly). At 17 km/s, the calculation to 4.37 ly gives approximately 77,000 years; to 4.24 ly it gives ~73,000–74,000 years. "75,000 years" is a reasonable middle estimate for the system at 4.37 ly, and "roughly" appropriately hedges it. The two essays use slightly different targets (Proxima vs. the system), so neither figure is wrong, but the internal tension across the collection is slightly distracting.

---

### Claim 3 — Voyager 1 current distance from Earth
> "less than 0.003 light-years out"

**Verdict: PASS (approximately).** As of ~2026, Voyager 1 is ~25 billion km from Earth. 0.003 ly = ~28.4 billion km. At ~25 billion km, Voyager is ~0.00264 ly out. The essay's "less than 0.003" is accurate.

---

### Claim 4 — 1% c travel time to Proxima
> "A ship aimed at Proxima Centauri at one percent of the speed of light… would arrive in about 430 years."

**Verdict: PASS.** 1% c = 2,998 km/s. Proxima is 4.24 ly × ~978 AU/ly = ~4,143 AU. At 3.16 AU/day (1% c) ≈ 3,594 days/AU… let's do it directly: 4.24 ly / 0.01c = 424 years. The essay says "about 430 years" — plausible rounding, probably using 4.3 ly or a slightly different baseline. Acceptable.

---

### Claim 5 — Biosphere 2 dates
> "eight people from September 1991 to September 1993"

**Verdict: PASS.** Sealed in 26 September 1991; exited September 1993 (two-year mission). Correct.

---

### Claim 6 — Biosphere 2 size
> "the 1.27-hectare sealed glass and steel structure"

**Verdict: PASS.** Wikipedia confirms 3.14 acres = 1.27 hectares. Accurate.

---

### Claim 7 — Biosphere 2 oxygen decline
> "oxygen levels began falling — from the Earth-normal 21 percent to 14.5 percent over two years, the equivalent of living at an altitude of 4,000 metres"

**Verdict: PASS.** Oxygen fell from 20.9% to 14.5% over 16 months (reaching minimum by early 1993 before injections). The "over two years" is slightly loose (the drop happened within 16 months, with injections in Jan and Aug 1993 to compensate), but the endpoint figure of 14.5% and altitude equivalent of ~4,000 m (sources say 4,080 m) are accurate.

---

### Claim 8 — Biosphere 2 concrete / CO2 mechanism
> "The culprit turned out to be the concrete in the structure itself: it was absorbing carbon dioxide faster than the biology was producing oxygen"

**Verdict: PASS.** This is the established explanation — uncured concrete reacted with CO2 (carbonation), locking it as calcium carbonate, which disrupted the expected O2/CO2 balance. Confirmed by multiple sources.

---

### Claim 9 — Biosphere 2 species die-off
> "Nineteen of the twenty-five vertebrate species introduced died out."

**Verdict: FLAG — NEEDS VERIFICATION 🟡.**  
This specific figure (19/25 vertebrate species) was not confirmed in the web searches conducted. Wikipedia and other sources describe vertebrate species die-offs but the "19 of 25" ratio could not be verified against primary sources in this review. The claim may be accurate but should be treated as uncertain without a direct cite. It does not appear in the Biosphere 2 Wikipedia article's main species die-off section.

---

### Claim 10 — Biosphere 2 morning glories
> "Morning glories, introduced as a food crop, escaped into the rainforest biome and crowded out other plants."

**Verdict: MOSTLY PASS — MINOR INACCURACY 🟡.**  
Morning glories were indeed invasive in Biosphere 2 and spread widely. However, sources describe them as introduced primarily for their flowers and spreading into the rainforest, not clearly as a "food crop." One source characterises them as having invaded and disrupted the ecosystem. The characterisation of them as initially a "food crop" may be an error or oversimplification — they are not a standard food crop (the tuber of *Ipomoea batatas*, sweet potato, is different from ornamental morning glories). This is a minor factual nuance but worth checking.

---

### Claim 11 — Cameron Smith MVP estimate
> "a minimum founder population of around 14,000 people would be needed to sustain genetic health over a 200-year voyage"

**Verdict: ORANGE 🟠 — VOYAGE DURATION WRONG.**  
Smith's 2014 study (Acta Astronautica, Vol. 97) modelled a **150-year voyage**, not 200 years. The study found 14,000–44,000 as the viable range, with 40,000 as the "safe" figure. The essay correctly cites the 14,000 minimum but misattributes the voyage duration as "200-year" when the paper assumed 150 years. The error doesn't invalidate the thrust of the argument but the voyage duration is a specific claim that is verifiably wrong.

**Correct:** "a minimum founder population of around 14,000 people would be needed to sustain genetic health over a **150-year** voyage"

---

### Claim 12 — Andrew Kennedy "wait calculation"
> "It was articulated clearly by the mathematician Andrew Kennedy in a 2006 paper in the Journal of the British Interplanetary Society"

**Verdict: PASS.** Kennedy published "Interstellar Travel: The Wait Calculation and the Incentive Trap of Progress" in JBIS vol. 59, 2006, pp. 239–246. Confirmed.

---

### Claim 13 — Voyager 1 current light-travel time
> "Voyager 1's current one-way light-travel time to Earth is about 23 hours."

**Verdict: PASS (approximately).** Voyager 1 is ~25 billion km from Earth. Light travels ~300,000 km/s; 25 billion km / 300,000 km/s = ~83,333 seconds = ~23.1 hours. Accurate.
