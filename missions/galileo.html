Galileo

First spacecraft to orbit Jupiter; atmospheric probe into Jupiter clouds

Galileo should have been crippled before it even arrived. Its main antenna stuck half-open on the way to Jupiter, cutting its data rate to a trickle — and the mission succeeded anyway, because engineers rewrote its software from Earth to squeeze the science through a tiny backup antenna. It became the first spacecraft to orbit Jupiter, dropped a probe into the giant planet’s clouds, and spent eight years touring the moons. Its greatest find emerged slowly from that persistence: the way Jupiter’s magnetic field wrapped around Europa could only be explained by a salty ocean beneath the ice — the strongest case yet that a moon of Jupiter might hold liquid water, and life. At the end it was flown into Jupiter on purpose, so it could never fall onto Europa and contaminate it.

Launched from Space Shuttle Atlantis after the long post-Challenger pause, Galileo braved the inner asteroid belt and conducted the first long-term study of Jupiter and its moons from orbit. It discovered Europa's induced magnetic field — strong evidence of a subsurface ocean — and watched Shoemaker-Levy 9 slam into Jupiter. The mission ended with a controlled plunge into Jupiter in 2003 to avoid contaminating icy moons that might harbour conditions for life.

EVENTS

  1. LAUNCH — STS-34 deploys Galileo and an inertial upper stage from Atlantis.
  2. JUPITER ORBIT INSERTION — Main engine burn captures Galileo into orbit; an 8-year tour of moon science begins.
