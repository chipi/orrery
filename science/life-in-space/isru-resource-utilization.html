ISRU — making oxygen, water, and fuel on the Moon and Mars

Hauling oxygen and propellant from Earth scales badly. ISRU — extracting them from local regolith, ice, and atmosphere — is the technology that turns surface stays from 30-day flag-planting into year-long living.

MOXIE was the proof-of-concept. Bolted to the Perseverance rover, the toaster-sized solid-oxide electrolysis unit ran 16 times between 2021 and 2023, pulling CO₂ from the 6 mbar Martian atmosphere, splitting it electrochemically at ~800 °C, venting CO, and producing pure O₂. Total yield: 122 g of oxygen, peak rate 12 g/hour. The point was not the quantity but the proof: that solid-oxide electrolysis works through dust storms, through Martian temperature swings, through the 6 mbar pressure regime. Scale-up factor for a crewed mission: ~200× by mass — a 1-tonne MOXIE-class plant running for 18-26 months pre-deployed before crew arrival produces ~25 tonnes of O₂, enough for ascent-vehicle oxidiser plus crew breathing margin. The architecture has matured enough that NASA's planning documents now treat Mars O₂ ISRU as committed baseline rather than research goal.

Lunar ISRU is centred on water-ice mining. The water exists — LCROSS impacted Cabeus crater in 2009 and detected H₂O/OH directly in the ejecta plume; Chandrayaan-1's Moon Mineralogy Mapper saw the spectral signature across the south polar PSRs in the same year; LRO/LAMP saw it in scattering UV; SOFIA confirmed it in non-polar latitudes in 2020. Quantity estimates vary widely (5-12% by weight in the highest-grade deposits), but enough that a 10-tonne capacity ice-mining excavator delivering to a 100-kg/day electrolyser produces ~50 kg/day of O₂ + 6 kg/day of H₂ — both useful for surface ops, both convertible to LH₂/LOX propellant for ascent. The engineering hard part is not the chemistry but the operating environment: PSR bottoms sit at 30-50 K (colder than Pluto), requiring excavator hardware that can survive without ever seeing the sun, and that ships and operates without thermal management systems sized for those temperatures.

On Mars, ISRU goes beyond oxygen. Water from subsurface ice (detected at high northern latitudes by Phoenix lander, mapped by MRO SHARAD radar, and now confirmed at lower latitudes by SWIM consortium analyses) is mined, melted, electrolysed to give H₂ and O₂. The H₂ feeds the Sabatier reaction (CO₂ + 4 H₂ → CH₄ + 2 H₂O) which produces methane for the methalox ascent vehicle, plus more water for recycling. The closed-loop chemistry: 25 tonnes of CH₄/LOX propellant + 5 tonnes of crew oxygen + 30 tonnes of recycled water, all from 60 tonnes of mined water-ice + pulled atmospheric CO₂, over 18 months of pre-deployment ops. The whole chain is testable on the surface before crew arrival — if the plant fails, the mission re-scopes to a flyby rather than risking a no-return situation.

ISRU also extends to construction materials. Lunar regolith — silicate-rich, with 40-50% by mass of bound oxygen — is being prototyped as 3D-printing feedstock for sintered structural blocks (ESA's Lunar Habitat project + NASA's 3D-Printed Habitat Challenge developed the techniques in lunar regolith simulants). Heating regolith to 1100 °C in vacuum produces a hard, low-porosity glassy material; with microwave sintering this can be done with a few kW of solar-electric energy. Mars regolith additionally supports brick-style architectures and concrete-like binders using Martian sulfur as the binding agent (sulfur is abundant on the surface). The point of all these material-ISRU paths is the same as for the propellant ISRU: avoid hauling mass from Earth where local resources can substitute, because the propellant cost of every imported kilogram dominates mission cost above all other terms.
