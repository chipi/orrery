Planetary Stats

How different is Jupiter from Earth, really? Eleven times the diameter. Two and a half times the surface gravity.

101 · zoom in

When you hear "Jupiter is the biggest planet", that's roughly true — but "big" hides multiple separate facts. Jupiter is 11× Earth's diameter, 318× Earth's mass, but only 2.53× Earth's surface gravity. Why doesn't 318× mass mean 318× gravity? Because gravity at the surface depends on both mass and the distance from the centre — and Jupiter's surface is 11× farther out than Earth's. The two effects almost cancel. Jupiter is huge, but standing on its 1-bar level (the conventional "surface" for a gas giant) you'd weigh 2.5× what you weigh on Earth — not 300×.

Mars is the opposite trade-off. Mars has only 11% of Earth's mass, but it has 53% of Earth's diameter. That makes its surface gravity 38% of Earth's — much higher than the mass ratio alone would predict, because you're closer to a smaller centre. The relationship that makes both cases sensible: surface gravity scales as mass / radius². For a planet of average density, gravity scales linearly with radius. Saturn (9.14× Earth's diameter) has surface gravity almost equal to Earth's (1.07 g) because Saturn is much less dense than Earth — Saturn is mostly hydrogen-helium, less than 0.7× water's density.

Atmospheric pressure: huge dynamic range. Mercury essentially zero. Mars 0.006 bar (a thin CO₂ wisp). Earth 1 bar. Venus 92 bar (about the pressure 900 m down in Earth's ocean — would crush you). Gas giants don't have a true "surface pressure" because there's no surface; the convention is to use the 1-bar level inside the atmosphere as the reference. Below that, pressures grow continuously into millions of bars deep in the interior.

Diameters relative to Earth: Mercury 0.38, Venus 0.95 (nearly Earth-sized), Earth 1.0, Mars 0.53, Jupiter 10.97, Saturn 9.14, Uranus 3.98, Neptune 3.86, Pluto 0.19. Surface gravities in Earth-g: Mercury 0.38, Venus 0.91, Earth 1.00, Mars 0.38, Jupiter 2.53, Saturn 1.07, Uranus 0.89, Neptune 1.14, Pluto 0.06. Notable surprises: Mars and Mercury share the same surface gravity (0.38 g) despite very different sizes, because their density × radius products land close. Uranus and Saturn both have lower-than-Earth gravity despite their giant sizes — both are dominated by H/He, much less dense than rocky Earth.

Rotation periods (sidereal, in hours): Mercury 1407.5 (essentially 58 Earth days for one full spin), Venus −5832.5 (retrograde, 243 days), Earth 23.93, Mars 24.62, Jupiter 9.93 (the fastest rotator — its day is under 10 hours despite its size, which is why Jupiter is visibly oblate; centrifugal forces flatten it), Saturn 10.66, Uranus −17.24 (retrograde), Neptune 16.11, Pluto −153.3 (retrograde). Solar days (sunrise to sunrise) differ from sidereal periods because the planet is also moving along its orbit — Venus's solar day is 117 Earth days even though its sidereal rotation is 243 days, because the retrograde spin partially cancels the orbital motion.
