Take a step back and look at what's in this orrery. Apollo 11 — Neil Armstrong's first footstep — happened in 1969. Voyager left for the outer planets in 1977. Hubble began seeing in 1990. Sojourner became the first wheels on Mars in 1997. Lunokhod had wheels on the Moon twenty-seven years before that. Chang'e 4 landed on the far side of the Moon in 2019 — the first lander ever to do so. Chandrayaan-3 placed wheels at the lunar south pole in 2023. Read those names again. NASA. ESA. Roscosmos. CNSA. ISRO. JAXA. CSA. SpaceIL. Eight agencies. Five continents. No single nation built this orrery. No single decade did. What you're looking at is the result of cooperative competition across seventy years — countries that disagree about almost everything on Earth agreeing, by their actions, to do the same hard thing in slightly different ways, and then sharing the data. Apollo was the first time we left. Artemis is the first time we plan to stay. A crewed Mars mission will be the first time we go to a place from which a same-week return is not physically possible. Each step on this ladder taught the next. Each agency on this ladder taught the others. The orrery in front of you is not a map of accomplishments. It is a map of what we now know is possible — because somebody has done it.
