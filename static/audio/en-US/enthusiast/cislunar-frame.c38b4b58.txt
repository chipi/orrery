Open the Fly screen and look at a Mars mission. Now look at a Moon mission. The two views look completely different — even though both spacecraft are doing the same thing: following an elliptical trajectory under gravity. The difference is the reference frame. Mars missions are drawn heliocentric. The Sun sits at the center of the view. Earth and Mars trace their orbits around it. The spacecraft's arc is a piece of an ellipse around the Sun, with perihelion at Earth's distance and aphelion out near Mars. This frame makes the geometry legible — you can see the spacecraft catching up to Mars as Mars itself moves. Moon missions are drawn cislunar. Earth sits at the center of the view. The Moon orbits around it. The spacecraft's arc — a translunar injection from low Earth orbit, cruise to lunar arrival, lunar orbit insertion — is drawn against Earth's reference frame, because everything important is happening in Earth's gravity well. Drawn heliocentric, this trajectory would be a tiny squiggle next to Earth's orbit around the Sun. You'd see nothing useful. Both views are correct. The spacecraft is doing the same thing in both — gravity does not change with your choice of frame. But what you can see changes. A Mars mission needs heliocentric scale to be readable. A Moon mission needs Earth-centered scale to be readable. Use the toolbar to switch frames on any mission. Watch what becomes visible in each. This is the same trick astronomers use when they switch between heliocentric, geocentric, and barycentric coordinates depending on what they want to compute. Choose the frame the problem lives in.
