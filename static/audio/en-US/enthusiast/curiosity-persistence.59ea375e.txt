Curiosity drives at a top speed of about four centimeters per second . A baby crawls faster. In a single sol — a Martian day — Curiosity covers an average of about thirty meters . Some days, much less. Some days, none — it stops, studies a rock with its laser, drills a hole, scans the dust. On bad days, the rover trips a fault, halts itself, waits to be told what went wrong. Now do the arithmetic. Curiosity landed in August 2012. As of now, it has been on Mars for over four thousand sols . In that time, it has driven about thirty-five kilometers . Thirty-five kilometers. A morning's bike ride. Stretched across the surface of another planet over twelve years. Alone. Every single sol of those twelve years, Curiosity has woken up at Martian sunrise. Checked its temperature. Checked its battery. Listened for instructions that took fourteen minutes to arrive. Then executed them. Then slept. There has been no day since August 6th, 2012, on which Curiosity did not exist alone on Mars. No relief shift. No vacation. No partner — until Perseverance arrived in 2021, more than three thousand kilometers away, on the other side of the planet. The car-sized robot you see on this map has been driving slower than a crawling child for longer than a child takes to grow into an adolescent. Every photograph it sends back is from a place no other being has ever stood. Every datapoint it returns is one humans on Earth could not gather without it. This is what persistence looks like at four centimeters per second.
