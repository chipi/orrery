Hold a conversation. Notice how quickly you respond to what the other person says. The gap between their last word and your first is usually less than half a second. When it stretches past two seconds, you both feel it — the air goes flat. Beyond ten seconds, you've stopped talking. You're taking turns leaving messages. The radio signal between Earth and the Moon takes one point three seconds , one way. Two point six seconds, round trip. Walter Cronkite's broadcasts during Apollo 11 had a noticeable pause every time Houston spoke to the crew. The astronauts spoke back. The pause held. The conversation worked — barely. Now imagine you tried to drive a Mars rover. One-way light time to Mars at average distance: about fourteen minutes . Round trip: about twenty-eight minutes . You push the joystick forward. Nothing happens for fourteen minutes. Then the rover starts moving. You see it moving fourteen minutes after that. By the time your "stop" command arrives, the rover has been driving — by your now-stale instructions — for half an hour. This is why there is no manual mode on the rover. There can't be. The rover has to think for itself in every short interval between the planning team's daily commands. Beyond a couple of light-seconds of distance, every spacecraft humanity has ever sent has had to become its own captain. The Moon is close. Mars is not.
