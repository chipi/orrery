Before Chang'e 4 could land on the far side of the Moon, China had to solve a basic communication problem. The far side of the Moon never faces Earth. A spacecraft on the surface there has no direct radio path to anywhere on this planet. The Moon itself is in the way. The solution was launched in May 2018. A relay satellite called Queqiao — pronounced cheh-chee-ow — Chinese for magpie bridge . Queqiao orbits the Earth-Moon L2 Lagrange point — a different L2 from the Sun-Earth L2 where JWST lives. This one sits sixty-five thousand kilometers beyond the Moon, along the Earth-Moon line. From that position, Queqiao has line-of-sight to both Earth and the Moon's far side simultaneously. It is the relay that forwards every signal from Chang'e 4 and the Yutu-2 rover back to ground stations on Earth. Why is it called the magpie bridge? In Chinese folklore, there are two lovers — a weaver girl and a cowherd — separated permanently by the Milky Way, allowed to meet only once a year, on the seventh night of the seventh month of the lunar calendar. When that night comes, all the world's magpies fly up and form a bridge of feathers across the river of stars so the lovers can cross. The lovers are the orbits of the Earth and the Moon. Queqiao is the bridge. Queqiao has been operating for over seven years now. Yutu-2 has been driving around the floor of the Von Kármán crater that whole time — over a kilometer of slow exploration, all of it transmitted home through the magpie bridge. Chang'e 6 — which returned the first samples from the far side in 2024 — used Queqiao 2, a successor satellite, in the same halo orbit. Every photograph the world has ever seen from the far side of the Moon arrived through this relay. Without it, the far side stays exactly as silent as it was in 1959 — before Luna 3 first photographed it.
