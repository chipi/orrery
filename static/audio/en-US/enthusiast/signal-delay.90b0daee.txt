When Curiosity radios home from Mars, the signal travels in a straight line at the speed of light. And it still takes anywhere from three minutes to twenty-two minutes to arrive. Three minutes when Mars and Earth are on the same side of the Sun, closest together. Twenty-two minutes when Mars is on the far side of the Sun, on the other side of the inner system. The radio isn't slow. The universe is large. This is why no human on Earth has ever driven Curiosity in real time. A joystick command — "turn left" — would arrive on Mars somewhere between three and twenty-two minutes after you sent it. By then the rover would already be eight meters past the rock you were trying to avoid. So Curiosity doesn't have a driver. It has a planner. Each Martian day — each sol — the team at JPL sends a sequence of commands the rover executes autonomously . Drive forward twelve meters. Stop. Take seven photos. Choose the safest of three onward paths. The rover sleeps. Earth sleeps. Mars rotates. And when both planets wake the next sol, fourteen minutes apart, the next set of commands flies through the dark to the rover that's already been working in the dark for sixteen hours. Fourteen minutes. Not slow. Just far.
