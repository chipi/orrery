Open the Orbits tab. Find the section called Vis-viva. What you're looking at is a single equation that determines the velocity of every spacecraft in this orrery, at every point along every orbit it flies. In symbols: v squared equals G times M, times the quantity two over r minus one over a. V for velocity. G for the gravitational constant. M for the mass of the central body — the Sun, for heliocentric orbits; Earth, for cislunar; the parent planet, for moons. R is the current distance from the central body. A is the orbit's semi-major axis — the average of the closest and furthest approaches. What it says, in plain words: your velocity at any point in an orbit depends only on two things — where you are right now, and the size of the orbit you're in. When you're at perihelion — closest to the central body — r is small, so the bracket is large, so v is large. You move fast. When you're at aphelion — furthest out — r is large, the bracket is small, v is small. You move slowly. Kepler's second law — equal areas in equal times — falls right out of this. Mercury at perihelion moves at almost fifty-nine kilometers per second. At aphelion, only thirty-nine. Earth varies between twenty-nine and thirty-one. Halley's Comet at perihelion races past the Sun at fifty-five kilometers per second. Out near Neptune, it crawls at one. Every transfer ellipse on the Plan screen is computed from this equation. Every porkchop contour. Every C three number. Every burn schedule for every mission you'll see fly on the Fly screen. One equation. Three variables. Six decades of spaceflight. This is what physics-first means.
