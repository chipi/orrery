In the late 1960s, a young engineer at JPL named Gary Flandro was running the math on how planetary positions repeat. He noticed that in the late 1970s, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune would all sit on the same side of the Sun, roughly in line. A single spacecraft could use Jupiter's gravity to slingshot to Saturn, Saturn's to slingshot to Uranus, Uranus's to reach Neptune. The alignment comes around once every one hundred and seventy-five years. The previous one had been during Thomas Jefferson's presidency. The next one will be in the twenty-second century. Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 launched in 1977 to catch it. Voyager 2 flew past Jupiter in 1979, Saturn in 1981, Uranus in 1986, and Neptune in 1989. It is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus or Neptune at all. Voyager 1 took a different option at Saturn — it traded Uranus and Neptune for a close look at Titan's atmosphere, then turned upward out of the ecliptic plane. Both spacecraft are still operating. Voyager 1 is the most distant human-made object — currently about twenty-four billion kilometers from the Sun. It crossed into interstellar space in 2012. Voyager 2 followed in 2018, on a different trajectory through the heliopause. Each spacecraft carries the Golden Record — a phonograph disc with recordings of natural sounds, music from twenty-seven cultures, and greetings in fifty-five languages. Carl Sagan led the team that chose what to put on it. The cover has instructions, in pulsar-position notation and hydrogen-atom diagrams, for any future intelligence that might find it. The spacecraft will keep going for tens of thousands of years. They will outlive the agencies that built them, the countries that funded them, and possibly the species that listened to the records. Most of the missions you will see in this catalogue were possible because Voyager went first.
