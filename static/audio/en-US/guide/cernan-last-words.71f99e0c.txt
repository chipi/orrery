Look at this map of the Moon. Every yellow marker is a place humans have been, or robots from Earth have been. Twelve of those markers were people. Tranquillity Base. Ocean of Storms. Fra Mauro. Hadley Rille. Descartes Highlands. Taurus-Littrow. Six Apollo landing sites, between July 1969 and December 1972. Three and a half years from first footstep to last footstep. Gene Cernan was the commander of Apollo 17. He was the eleventh person to walk on the Moon, and the second-to-last back into the lunar module. The very last person was Cernan himself — he was the commander, so he climbed up the ladder behind his crewmate, took one last look at the surface, and then he said this: "As we leave the Moon at Taurus-Littrow, we leave as we came, and, God willing, as we shall return — with peace, and hope for all mankind." "Godspeed the crew of Apollo Seventeen." That was December 14th, 1972. Gene Cernan died in 2017, having lived almost his entire adult life as the most recent person to set foot on another world . As of the moment you are listening to this, more than fifty years have passed. In that half century, three Mars rovers have been built and sent. Two space stations have been continuously occupied. One probe has gone interstellar . And nobody has been back to the Moon. Artemis II is in flight planning. If it flies on schedule, Cernan's title — last person on the Moon — will pass to someone else. Until that happens, every time you look at this map, twelve markers represent a closed chapter. A chapter we are still trying to open again.
