This is Mars. Every marker on the surface is the resting place of a spacecraft humans built on Earth and sent here. Mariner 4 flew by in 1965. It took the first close-up photographs of any other planet. Mars 2, a Soviet lander, crashed here in 1971. Mars 3 made the first soft landing — but transmitted for only twenty seconds. Viking 1 and Viking 2, in 1976, took the first photographs from the surface. Click Pathfinder's marker, in Ares Vallis. Pathfinder landed in 1997. It carried Sojourner — about the size of a microwave oven — the first wheels to ever drive on another planet. Sojourner drove about a hundred meters in total. Then the lander died, and Sojourner with it. Click Curiosity, in Gale Crater. Curiosity landed in 2012. It is the size of a small car. It has driven over thirty kilometers across the floor of Gale Crater and up the lower slopes of Mount Sharp — and as of this moment, it is still moving. Click Perseverance, in Jezero Crater. Perseverance is Curiosity's cousin — same chassis design, different instruments, different mission. It carries the first helicopter ever flown on another planet, Ingenuity. Ingenuity made seventy-two flights before it broke a rotor blade in early 2024. Look at the orbiters around Mars. Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter. Mars Express. MAVEN. Mars Odyssey. ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter. Mangalyaan from India. Hope from the United Arab Emirates. Tianwen-1 from China. Eleven orbiters work, or recently worked, in the Martian sky. They photograph the surface, sniff the atmosphere, relay signals from the rovers, watch the dust storms. Click any surface marker and the right panel opens with the mission's history, science return, and gallery. Where a rover has driven, the traverse is drawn on the map as a thin line. Zoom in to Curiosity in Gale Crater to see its full route since 2012 — every sol's drive recorded as a faint trace across the plain. Twenty surface missions have arrived here over sixty years. Most of them are still here — silent, but there. Look at the map. Read the dates. Notice how Mars has slowly stopped being a place humans only fly past.
