The grid in front of you is the Mission Catalogue. Forty-two missions, from the first robotic probes of the 1960s to the crewed flights planned for next decade. Every card has a launch date, a destination, an operating agency, a status, and a one-sentence description. Use the filters at the top. Filter by status — flown, active, planned, concept. Filter by destination — Moon, Mars, Venus, outer planets, deep space. Filter by agency — NASA, ESA, JAXA, ISRO, CNSA, Roscosmos, SpaceX, SpaceIL. Try this combination. Filter status equals failed. You'll see Mars 2. Schiaparelli. Beresheet. Hakuto-R. Vikram from Chandrayaan-2. Hitomi. This is the redemption-arc layer of spaceflight. Every one of these missions is paired in the catalogue with its successor. Vikram CY-2 failed in 2019. Chandrayaan-3 landed Vikram's twin at the lunar south pole in 2023. Beresheet hit the surface in 2019. Beresheet 2 is in development. Apollo 1 killed three astronauts on the launch pad in 1967. Apollo 11 walked on the Moon two and a half years later. Click any mission card. The right panel opens with the full record — launch vehicle, payload mass, crew or instruments, key events, gallery of images, links to read further. Where the mission has a destination on a planetary surface, the explore button jumps you to that surface with the landing site already selected. Look at the timeline. Notice the gaps. Between Apollo 17 in 1972 and Luna 24 in 1976, the Moon was visited four times. Then nothing landed there for nearly thirty years. Then Chang'e 1 in 2007 reopened the visit list, and the line is now thicker than it has ever been. Forty-two missions is not the whole story.
